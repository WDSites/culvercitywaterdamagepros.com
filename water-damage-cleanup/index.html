First 48 hours: what cleanup involves

Cleanup is the stage where the home goes from wet to clean. Crews remove soaked carpet pad, ruined drywall and debris, then wash and disinfect what stays. Anything that held water for long gets a closer look, because wet porous materials hold odor and bacteria.

Cleanup overlaps with mitigation, since removal of wet materials helps the drying process. If you have not yet had a moisture check, mention that when you call so the visit covers both. Evening and weekend calls are routine, and a same-day visit is offered when a crew is free.

What stays and what goes

Not every wet item is lost, and not every dry-looking one is safe. Crews decide by material and by water category, using the standard that guides this trade.

Usually saved

Hard surfaces such as sealed tile, concrete, metal and solid wood furniture can often be cleaned and dried.

Often removed

Carpet pad, soaked insulation, fiberboard cabinets and drywall that stayed wet are common removals.

Depends on the water

Carpet and rugs can sometimes be cleaned after clean water but are usually discarded after sewage or floodwater.

Personal belongings

Photographs, documents and keepsakes can sometimes be dried or cleaned. Ask before throwing them out.

A typical cleanup sequence

The sequence below is the usual order. Your crew may change it if a safety issue or a delicate material calls for it.

  1. Walk-through and photos. The crew documents the damage and agrees on what is being removed before any cutting starts.
  2. Contents sorting. Belongings are separated into dry, cleanable and discard groups, with a list you can share with your insurer.
  3. Removal. Soaked pad, flooring and drywall are cut out, bagged and carried to a debris container or the crew vehicle.
  4. Cleaning and sanitizing. Remaining surfaces are washed and treated with an antimicrobial suited to the material and water category.
  5. Odor control. Air scrubbers or other equipment are used when smells linger after the wet materials are gone.

When cleanup becomes an emergency

Cleanup is an emergency when the water is not clean. A sewage backup, a toilet overflow with solids, or floodwater from outside carries bacteria and should be handled by a crew in protective gear. Stay out of the water, keep children and pets away and avoid touching wet surfaces with bare hands.

If water has reached an outlet, a light fixture or an appliance, leave the electrical side alone. Call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas risk before you call the restoration line. Once the area is safe, the dispatcher can send a crew on the same day when one is available.

What sets the price of cleanup

No honest number can come from a web page, because the scope is decided in your home. These factors drive the final price.

Amount of material removed

More carpet, drywall and cabinetry removed means more labor and disposal.

Water category

Sewage and floodwater cleanup needs more protection and more cleaning steps.

Contents handling

Sorting, packing and cleaning belongings adds time and is itemized.

Odor treatment

Persistent smells may need extra equipment or a second visit.

What to tell your insurer

Many policies cover sudden and accidental water damage and exclude flood and gradual leaks. Read the exclusions in your policy, because the California Department of Insurance notes that flood and wear and tear are among the losses usually excluded. Ask your insurer which category your event fits.

Keep a simple list of what was removed, with photos, so your claim records match your cleanup records. Questions about price or scope are welcome on the 24/7 line at (424) 550-5662, and the crew explains scope and price before work starts.
