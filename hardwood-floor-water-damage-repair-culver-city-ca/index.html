Hardwood Floor Water Damage Repair in Culver City, CA

Hardwood floors can often be saved after water damage if they are dried slowly and the moisture source is fixed first. The common signs are cupping, where board edges rise, crowning, where the center rises, gaps, staining and a musty smell. Do not sand or refinish until readings show the floor and subfloor are dry. This guide explains what to do in the first days and when repair gives way to replacement.

By the Culver City Water Damage Pros team · October 27, 2026 · Flooring

How water changes hardwood

Wood absorbs moisture from the bottom and the edges first. The underside of a board swells, the edges rise and the floor cups. If the surface is sealed, water enters through gaps between boards and under the floor.

That is why a floor can look flat after a leak and then cup days later, once the moisture has traveled. Wait to judge the damage until the moisture source is stopped and the floor has dried at a steady pace.

Signs of damage and what they usually mean

Different shapes tell different stories about the water.

What to do in the first days

These steps help a floor recover, and they are safe for homeowners.

  1. Stop the water. Fix or isolate the leak. Mop up any surface water.
  2. Lift items. Remove rugs, mats and furniture so air can reach the floor.
  3. Call for readings. A moisture meter shows how wet the boards and the subfloor are, and that guides the drying plan.
  4. Dry slowly. Dehumidification and gentle airflow are used. Heating a floor quickly can warp it.
  5. Wait before sanding. Sanding while the floor is still wet leads to gaps and re-cupping.

Can the floor be sanded and refinished?

If the boards are structurally sound after drying, sanding and refinishing can level mild cupping and restore the finish. The floor needs enough wear layer left, and it must be dry throughout.

A refinishing crew will measure moisture first and often waits days or weeks after drying to confirm the floor is stable. Ask how they confirm that and what they do if the floor re-cups. Water damage repair on a floor usually follows water mitigation, because drying is the foundation of any repair.

When replacement is the right call

Replacement makes sense when boards are cracked, stained black, loose or severely cupped after drying. It also makes sense when a floor stayed wet for so long that mold or rot has set in.

A repair crew will remove the damaged section and replace it with matching boards, then blend the finish. Matching an older floor takes care, and a test patch is a good idea. The water damage repair page explains how scope and price are set before work begins.

Subfloor and underlayment

The subfloor is often the hidden problem. Plywood and underlayment that soaked can stay wet long after the boards look dry, so readings on both layers matter. If the subfloor is damaged, it is cut back to sound material before new boards go down.

Any wood floor over a slab or a raised foundation has its own moisture story. A crew checks the layers and does not guess based on the surface alone.

Why hardwood reacts slowly and then all at once

Wood takes on water gradually and gives it up gradually. A floor can look fine on the first day and cup or crown a week later as moisture works through the boards. That delay is why a moisture reading matters more than how the floor looks.

Finish also changes the story. A sealed top surface slows water getting in from above but can trap moisture that arrives from below, so the board dries unevenly and stresses the glue or nails holding it.

Habits that protect a hardwood floor

These help before and after a leak.

  • Wipe up spills from dishwashers, refrigerators and plants right away.
  • Check under sinks and behind appliances for slow drips twice a year.
  • Keep indoor humidity steady with ventilation and a dehumidifier when needed.
  • Use mats at entries and under planters.
  • Do not seal a damp floor with a new finish.

Culver City notes for floor repairs

The Building Safety Division's permit list names remodel or alteration, termite or dry rot repair and wall replacement among work that needs a permit, while it lists painting and minor sheetrock repair as exempt. Floor refinishing is not named on that list, so ask the division about subfloor replacement and anything that touches framing.
