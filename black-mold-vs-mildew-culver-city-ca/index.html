Black Mold vs Mildew in Culver City, CA: How to Tell the Difference

Mildew is usually flat and powdery on surfaces like shower tile, while mold grows deeper into materials and can look fuzzy or slimy in many colors. Color alone cannot tell you what you have, and both grow where moisture lingers. The practical steps are the same: find the water, dry the area and remove or clean what the growth has damaged.

By the Culver City Water Damage Pros team · October 27, 2026 · Mold

What people mean by black mold

Black mold is a common name for dark mold growth, and it is often used for one species in particular. Growth that looks black can come from many kinds of mold, and growth that looks green or gray can be the same species at a different stage.

That is why color is a poor guide. Only a trained inspector or laboratory testing can identify what is present. For a homeowner, the useful question is not the color but how much material is affected and where the moisture is coming from.

Mildew and mold compared

These descriptions are general and not a diagnosis.

Why the difference matters less than the moisture

The EPA states that the key to mold control is moisture control. Whether you call a patch mildew or mold, the cause is a wet or damp surface, and the fix starts with the water.

A bathroom with poor ventilation, a leaky window seal, a plumbing drip or a flooded floor will keep growing the same growth until the moisture is stopped. Cleaning the surface without fixing the source is a short-term fix.

What to do about either one

The basic steps apply to mildew and to mold.

  1. Fix the water source. Repair leaks, improve ventilation and dry wet materials.
  2. Clean small hard surfaces. Wipe tile and sealed surfaces with detergent and water, wearing gloves and a mask.
  3. Remove porous materials. Drywall, insulation and ceiling tiles with growth are usually removed, not cleaned.
  4. Dry and recheck. Dehumidify and watch for return.
  5. Call for help when growth spreads. Large or recurring growth needs inspection and containment.

Testing and what it can and cannot tell you

Testing can show what is present and how much, and clearance testing after remediation can check results. It does not replace fixing moisture, and it does not answer health questions.

Health concerns belong to a physician. A contractor cannot diagnose illness or make claims about health from a test result. The mold remediation page explains the inspection, containment, removal and drying that follow when mold is confirmed.

After a leak or flood

If growth appears after a leak or flood, it means materials stayed wet. The EPA says water-damaged areas and items should be dried within 24 to 48 hours. If you missed that window, a moisture check can tell whether walls and floors are still wet.

Water mitigation dries the structure and reduces the chance that growth returns. If the water was contaminated, treat the area as a flood damage job.

What to photograph for your records

Photos help an inspector or an insurer later.

  • Wide shots that show the room and where the growth sits.
  • Close-ups with a coin or ruler for scale.
  • The suspected moisture source, such as a pipe, vent or window.
  • Any water stains or bubbled paint nearby.
  • A dated note on when you first saw it.

Why the label matters to labs, not to your plan

Laboratories sort mold by genus and species, and that information helps in rare cases. For a homeowner deciding what to do next, the label rarely changes the plan, because the response to visible growth is to find the water and remove or clean the affected material.

That is also why fear based on color is not useful. Treat all growth seriously, keep the area dry and ask a qualified inspector what the situation needs.
