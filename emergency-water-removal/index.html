When standing water is an emergency

Water you can see on the floor is only part of the problem. It runs into wall cavities, under baseboards and down through subfloors, and it keeps spreading while you wait. Removing it quickly limits how far it travels and how many materials need replacing.

Treat it as an emergency if it covers more than a small area, if it is still flowing, or if it came from a drain, toilet or outside. Call the 24/7 line from a dry spot. A crew can often be sent the same day, and the dispatcher will tell you what to do in the meantime.

If water is still spreading, do this first

These steps buy time. They are not a substitute for a crew, but they reduce the amount of water the crew has to remove.

Stop the source

Close the main valve or the fixture supply. For a water heater, close the cold supply line at the top of the tank.

Think power first

If water is near outlets or a panel, do not step in it. Call the utility or 911 before you call the restoration line.

Lift what you can

Move electronics, rugs and furniture legs onto foil or foam blocks if you can do it without touching water.

Document it

Photograph the water line on walls and the source of the leak before anything is moved.

How water is removed

The equipment depends on how deep the water is and what is underneath it. The crew picks the method on site.

  1. Submersible pumps. Used when water is deep enough to sit above the floor. Hoses carry it to a drain or outside.
  2. Truck-mounted or portable extraction. Used for shallow water, carpet and pad, and areas where a pump cannot reach.
  3. Squeegee and wet vac work. Used on hard floors and at edges, which finish the visible removal.
  4. Moisture check afterward. Meters check walls, subfloor and framing so the crew knows how much hidden water remains.

Burst pipe water damage

A burst pipe can release a very large volume of water in a short time, especially in a wall or ceiling where you cannot see it. The signs are a sudden wet spot, the sound of running water with every fixture off, or a water meter that keeps moving.

Close the main shutoff, open a low faucet to drain the lines, and call. The crew removes standing water, opens affected cavities and starts drying. A plumber is still needed to repair the pipe, so book one the same day while the crew works on water removal.

If the broken line is a main supply, remember that replacing water piping needs a city permit, while stopping a leak without replacing pipe does not. Ask the Building Safety Division at (310) 253-5800 when you plan the repair.

Insurance and burst pipes

A burst pipe is usually treated as sudden and accidental damage, which many homeowners policies cover. Gradual leaks from worn pipes and neglect often are not, and flood from outside is usually excluded. The California Department of Insurance advises reading the exclusions in your policy.

A flood insurance policy typically does not cover water from indoor sources such as burst pipes, according to the department, so your homeowners policy is the first place to check. Ask your insurer before cleanup starts, and keep photos and readings.

Culver City storms and emergency water

The city's own storm notice from February 2024 warned of peak rain rates that could reach half an inch to one inch an hour. It listed sandbags at Fire Station One downtown, the headquarters station at 9600 Culver Blvd and the City Yard at 9505 Jefferson Blvd. Water that rises in a driveway or garage during a storm is exactly what this page covers.

The Culver City Fire Department advises residents not to enter flooded subterranean garages or other below-grade areas, and says water as shallow as six inches can sweep a person away. If water is rising, call 911 for any life safety risk before you call a restoration crew.
